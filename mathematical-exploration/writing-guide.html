Is a Hershey’s Kiss actually a cone?

Follow one Mathematical Exploration from a first question to a conclusion. Each part shows what to put on the page and why it helps a reader follow your thinking. Use the example to make decisions about your own write-up.

Question and scope

In your write-up Give the reader a focused question and say what evidence you’ll use to answer it. Explain what you mean by a better fit.

Example passage

The side of a Hershey’s Kiss looks rounded, even though it tapers to a tip. I wanted to see how closely a straight-sided cone would match it. I use the outline to compare how closely a cone and a stack of truncated cones fit the shape and how their volume estimates differ. I compare their radii at heights I leave out when building the models.

Why it helps The reader knows what you’re testing and where the evidence comes from. A focused question and a clear order support Presentation.

Diagram, measurements and assumptions

In your write-up Show where your readings come from. Define your variables, give the units and explain the assumption that lets you go from a flat outline to a volume.

Example methods passage

In the outline, z is the height above the base. I set the full height to 1 and use that same length unit for horizontal widths. I call the width w and use r = w/2 as the radius. At z = 0.50, a width of 0.70 gives a radius of 0.35. For the volume models I assume each horizontal slice is a circle centred on the same vertical axis.

One cone as a first model

In your write-up Explain why the first model makes sense. Say which readings set it and show how you used them.

Example model passage

I start with a cone because it connects the base edge and tip with a straight line. It uses a base radius of 0.50 and a height of 1. Its radius shrinks at a constant rate, so rcone(z) = 0.50(1 − z).

Vcone = πr²h/3 = π(0.50)²(1)/3 = π/12 ≈ 0.2618 cubic height units.

Why it helps The reader can see why a cone is a reasonable starting point and check how the estimate follows from the inputs. This is evidence for Use of mathematics.

Where the cone misses

In your write-up Point to a specific mismatch. Explain what it tells you about the first model and how it shaped your next measurement.

Example reflection passage

Halfway up, the cone gives a radius of 0.25. The outline gives 0.35, so the cone is 0.10 height units too narrow on each side there. From the base to halfway, the cone narrows faster than the outline.

I chose another width at z = 0.375, halfway between two readings in the lower half of the outline. The cone’s edge was well inside the outline there, so I wanted to see if adding a reading would help the stack follow it. I left z = 0.4375 out so I could test the revised stack at a different height nearby.

Why it helps Diagnosing the gap and planning another check show Reflection. Explaining why you chose that particular measurement shows Personal engagement through a mathematical decision.

Stacked truncated cones

In your write-up Explain how the new model works and what changed. Show one calculation in full, then use a table for the repeated pieces.

Example methods passage and calculation

I connect consecutive radii with straight segments. Rotating each segment around the vertical axis gives a truncated cone. The top piece is a cone with an upper radius of zero. The outline width at z = 0.375 is 0.824, so its radius is 0.412. This splits one piece of the original stack into two.

For the bottom piece, the height Δz is 0.25, the lower radius R is 0.50 and the upper radius r is 0.46.
V1 = πΔz(R² + Rr + r²)/3
= π(0.25)[(0.50)² + (0.50)(0.46) + (0.46)²]/3
= π(0.25)(0.2500 + 0.2300 + 0.2116)/3
≈ 0.1811 cubic height units.

Comparison, conclusion and limitations

In your write-up Test the models on the same unused readings. Say what changed, answer your question and explain what you’d need to test real volume.

Example results passage

The cone estimates 0.2618 cubic height units. The original stack estimates 0.3791, and the stack with the extra width estimates 0.3813. To check the fit, I compare their radii at four heights I left out when building the models.

At z = 0.4375, the original stack’s radius error is 0.0065 and the revised stack’s is 0.0030. The new width helped at that nearby height. Checking at z = 0.375 would only show that the revised stack passes through a point I used to build it.

Example conclusion and limitations

The revised stack fits the outline better than the cone at the heights I checked. That comparison only tests one side view, so the volume estimates still depend on my assumption that the slices are circular.

To check the volume of an actual chocolate, I’d measure its height and use a calibrated side view. A top view or side views taken as I turn it would help me check whether the slices are circular. I’d then compare the scaled estimate with an independent volume measurement, such as water displacement, and report the uncertainty.

Why it helps Readers can see the difference between fitting the picture, estimating a volume and checking that estimate against a real object. Connecting the limitation to a specific new measurement makes the Reflection useful.

Optional HL curve and integral

In your write-up Name the fitting points, explain why the area expression works and check the smooth curve on readings it didn’t use.

Example HL methods passage

I fit a quadratic to the outline’s radii at z = 0, 0.50 and 1, with radii 0.50, 0.35 and 0. This gives r(z) = 0.50 − 0.10z − 0.40z². With circular slices, each cross-sectional area is π[r(z)]².

Vcurve = π∫01 (0.50 − 0.10z − 0.40z²)² dz = 0.122π ≈ 0.3833 cubic height units.

Example HL reflection passage

The smooth curve’s mean error of 0.0066 is slightly larger than the revised stack’s 0.0055 on these readings. The curve used three fitting points and the revised stack used six. To compare the modelling methods more fairly, I’d fit both using the same readings and check them on a separate set. A smooth fit still tells me only about this side view.

Why it helps The integral follows from a stated assumption, and the test gives the reader a way to judge what the extra math added.

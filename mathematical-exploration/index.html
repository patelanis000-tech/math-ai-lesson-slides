The Mathematical Exploration

An individual written investigation of mathematics around a topic you choose.

You pose a question or set an aim, use mathematics to investigate it, and explain what your results tell you.

Your teacher assesses the report. The IB moderates the assessment.

Is a Hershey’s Kiss actually a cone?

Look at the side view. Where would your cone miss the outline?

  1. Sketch the side view and draw the cone you’d use. Mark its base and tip.
  2. Mark where your cone would sit inside or outside the chocolate outline.
  3. Pick one height where a width would help you check your guess. Write why.

Keep your sketch. On the next slide we’ll use widths from the practice outline.

One cone or a stack of pieces?

Use the same side outline. z is height from the base. We measure heights and widths in the same unit, with the full height set to 1. Take half of each width to get its radius.

What the IA criteria look like in this example

A Presentation
State the cone question. Show the outline and width readings, then the models, a check and your next step.
B Mathematical communication
Label height and width on the diagram. Show that a width of 0.92 gives a radius of 0.46. Give volumes in cubic height units.
C Personal engagement
Explain why you chose an extra width where a model seemed to miss. Show what changed when you added it.
D Reflection
Use an unused width to check the outline. Explain what a side view leaves open and what you’d measure next.
E Use of mathematics
Show a cone or truncated-cone calculation and explain how adding the pieces estimates the whole volume.

Show this kind of evidence in your own words, wherever it fits in your investigation.

Did the extra width help?

Try a new check

Halfway up, the cone gives a radius of 0.25 and the outline gives 0.35. The stack uses that width. It also matches the extra width you chose, so test at a different height.

Use the choice you made on the volume slide, then compare the two errors here.

What would check the volume?

The side outline helps us judge where each edge fits. The volume estimates also assume circular slices.

Measure the real chocolate’s full height to set the scale. Then measure its displaced volume independently, for example by fully submerging it in a graduated cylinder and reading the rise in water level.

From one test to a full exploration

The IB guide suggests about 12–20 double-spaced pages, including graphs and diagrams. The bibliography is separate. Start with a cone and mark where it misses. Measure one extra width there and rebuild the stack. Check at another height you haven’t used, then plan a physical check of the volume.

Use a graph, table or explanation when it helps readers see how you made a decision or what your result means.

Two ways into a mind map

Put either starting point in the middle. Draw branches for questions, possible data or models, and math you could try.

Start with something you’re curious about

  1. The shape of a Hershey’s Kiss
  2. Is a Hershey’s Kiss actually a cone?
  3. A side view, width readings and volume models

Start with math you’d like to use

  1. Functions and graphs
  2. Where does changing one quantity affect another?
  3. Rectangle width and area with a fixed perimeter

Add as many branches as are useful to you. If you already have a topic, map the assumptions it depends on.

What makes an idea workable?

A focused question
Can you say what you’ll measure, compare or model? Name the relevant variables or conditions.
Data or a model you can access
Can you get a few relevant values today? If you’re building a model, can you generate its first outputs?
Math you can explain
Can you show what the math does and why it helps answer your question?
Room for interpretation
What would the result mean? What might limit it or change your decision?

Pick the uncertainty that matters most. Build your small test around it.

A small test for your idea

  • Explore a branchCollect or generate a few values. Make a quick graph, table or calculation and see what it reveals about your question.
  • Compare promising ideasTry the same small check on each idea you’re comparing. For example, find a usable sample and calculate one quantity for each.
  • Stress-test an assumptionPick the assumption most likely to make your topic unworkable. Say what would change your mind, then vary an input, try a measurement or check a prediction.

Choose a route that fits where you are. A sketch of a plan is a start. Go far enough to record a result today.

AI in this lesson

Think independently first. Write your own idea and the assumption you want to check.

With AI · optional

Ask AI for questions that help you stress-test the idea you already have.

“I want to model a chocolate from a side view. Ask me questions that would help me check my assumption that the slices are circular.”

Without AI

Ask yourself the same questions. Which assumption is most fragile? What would change my mind? What can I measure or calculate now?

You choose the final question, do the analysis and write the reflection. Keep AI out of those three tasks. Verify any sources and claims independently.

SIS student AI guide IB AI guidance

48 min live · 16 min solo

Your first independent test

  1. Write a first version of your question or the assumption you want to check.
  2. Try it now. Measure, use a small data sample or generate model outputs. Label where the values came from and include units.
  3. Show one piece of math. Make a table or graph, calculate a value or compare two results.
  4. Record what happened. What did the result tell you? If you hit a barrier, record what you tried and what you could test instead.

A paper-and-pencil starting point

How does width affect the area of a rectangle with a 20 cm perimeter?

Sketch rectangles with widths 1 cm, 3 cm and 5 cm. Use 2(length + width) = 20 to find each length. Calculate and compare their areas.

What pattern do you see? What width would you try next to investigate it?

Keep the calculation, graph or attempted check in your rough notes. If you’re catching up solo, go straight to the closing questions.

Optional HL · about 5 min

Would a smooth curve do better?

Use the same side view. Fit a quadratic radius to just three heights: base, halfway and tip. Their radii are 0.50, 0.35 and 0.

Predict whether the curve will beat the original stack at points held out of both fits. What would count as a better fit?

Second live block · 45 min

A follow-up test or a change of direction

  1. 5 min. Reopen your notes and pick one thing the first check left uncertain, or try the optional HL example.
  2. 34 min. Work on your own. Repeat a measurement, change an input, narrow the question and test it, or try a different idea. Keep the new evidence next to the first result.
  3. 6 min. Use the closing questions to decide what you might do next.

Continue on your own while others talk. Discussion or teacher feedback can help if available.

Your rough notes

Keep the evidence beside your answers in your rough notes. It’s fine to have a few ideas you’re still weighing up.

  1. What did you try?
  2. What did it tell you?
  3. Will you keep going, narrow it, or try something else?

Bring your rough notes to the next math class. Be ready to tell me what you tried, what you found, and what you might do next.
